## 1. The detour around a frozen wall

A layer normally computes `h = W·x`, and normal fine-tuning computes an update and overwrites `W`: `W_new = W + ΔW`. **LoRA leaves `W` frozen forever** and learns `ΔW` as a separate, cheap object sitting *beside* it:

![LoRA structure: input x flows through two parallel paths. The top path goes through the frozen 4096 by 4096 weight matrix W. The bottom path goes through A, which squeezes 4096 dimensions down to 8, then B, which expands 8 back to 4096. The two outputs are added together. Dashed means frozen with no gradients stored; the solid accent A and B are the only weights that change — 65K of 16.8M, about 0.4%.](/images/finetuning/finetuning-lora-structure.svg)

The output is `h = W·x + (α/r)·B·A·x`. Matrix products apply right-to-left, so in `B·A·x` the `A` nearest `x` acts first — which is exactly the left-to-right order of the picture: **`A` squeezes 4096 numbers down to `r` (=8), and `B` expands `r` back up to 4096.** That narrow `r`-wide waist is the whole trick. Four consequences worth stating precisely, because most misconceptions live here:

- **`W` gets no gradient.** That kills both the gradient tensor *and* the optimizer state for it — the real memory win, and the reason we'll see a 7B model fit on a single consumer card.
- **`B` starts at zeros, `A` at small random values.** So `B·A·x = 0` at step 0 and the model behaves *exactly* like the base model on the first step. Both-zero would be a dead fixed point (A's gradient depends on B and vice versa), so the asymmetry is forced, not stylistic.
- After training, `W + B·A` is just a matrix. **Merge it and ship a normal model with zero added inference latency**, or keep it separate and hot-swap adapters (more on that at the end).

## 2. What "rank" actually means — the times table

A rank-1 matrix is a multiplication table. Put one row of `A` across the top and one column of `B` down the side; the interior is their products.

![A rank-one matrix shown as a multiplication table. Four numbers 2, 1, 3, 1 run down the left edge and four numbers 1, 0, 2, 3 run across the top; the sixteen interior cells are their products. Every interior row is the top row rescaled by the number on its left — doubled, as-is, tripled, as-is. Eight numbers on the edges generate all sixteen inside; at real scale, one such rank-1 slice has 4096 plus 4096 equals 8,192 edge numbers generating 16.8M interior entries.](/images/finetuning/finetuning-rank-table.svg)

Notice what's *impossible*: no row can do its own thing. Every row is the same pattern, louder or quieter. That constraint is exactly what buys the compression — **16 cells, 8 free numbers.** And "rank 8" means eight such tables, stacked and added, so a row of ΔW can be any *mix* of 8 patterns — far more expressive than one, far less than 4096 independent rows. (Eight tables of 8,192 edge numbers each is exactly the **65K** trainable parameters from §1's figure: `8 × 8,192 = 65,536`.)

### Low-rank is not sparse

This is the single most common misconception, so it gets its own heading. **ΔW is fully dense** — every entry nonzero. When you merge, *every* weight in the layer differs from the original. The restriction isn't on *how many* entries change; it's that all the changes are generated by `r` coordinated patterns instead of varying independently. **Cheap to describe, not narrow in reach.** "LoRA works because most weights don't need to change" is wrong twice over.

## 3. Three things, three names

It's easy to blur the vocabulary. The edges are the stored, trained parameters; the interior is what they *generate*.

![Anatomy of a rank-one slice. The four numbers across the top are one row of A, stored and trained. The four numbers down the left are one column of B, stored and trained. The sixteen interior numbers are their products, forming one rank-one slice of delta W — computed on the fly and never stored.](/images/finetuning/finetuning-three-names.svg)

`A` holds `r` rows, `B` holds `r` columns. Pair them up — row 1 with column 1, and so on — and each pair generates one table. **During training the interior is never computed even once:** the layer evaluates `B·(A·x)`, two small matrix-vector products. The full table only materializes if you *deliberately* merge.

| Thing | What it is | Stored? |
|---|---|---|
| a row of A | the top edge | yes, trained |
| a column of B | the left edge | yes, trained |
| "the table" | the interior — one rank-1 slice of ΔW | **no, never materialized** |

## 4. Where `r = 8` came from

Nobody derived it. It's a hyperparameter, and 8 got sticky for two unglamorous reasons:

1. The original paper ([Hu et al., 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.09685)) swept `r = 1, 2, 4, 8, 64` on GPT-3 175B and the curves were nearly flat — `r=1` matched `r=64` on several tasks. 8 looked like a safe margin.
2. Hugging Face [PEFT](https://github.com/huggingface/peft) made `r=8` the default in `LoraConfig`, and millions of fine-tunes never changed it. A genuine cargo cult downstream of a reasonable default.

**Why small `r` works at all:** pretraining already installed the machinery — grammar, facts, reasoning circuits. Fine-tuning mostly *re-weights* what's already there ("be terse," "emit JSON," "adopt this register"), and that kind of change is low-rank by nature. The precedent is [Aghajanyan et al., 2020](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.13255), who showed BERT could be fine-tuned well by optimizing a few hundred parameters in a random low-dimensional subspace; LoRA turned that observation into a clean, mergeable architecture.

| Task distance from pretraining | Typical r |
|---|---|
| Style, tone, output format, instruction-following | 8–16 |
| New domain vocabulary (legal, medical, credit) | 16–32 |
| New language, or genuinely new factual knowledge | 64–256, or reconsider LoRA |

Honest caveat: **rank is a real ceiling.** LoRA underperforms full fine-tuning when *installing new knowledge* rather than resurfacing existing behavior. It learns less, but it also forgets less — frozen `W` acts as a regularizer. (Part 2 is the whole argument that a factual gap is the wrong thing to reach for fine-tuning to fix.)

## 5. The gain: `α / r`

The adapter's output is multiplied by a single scalar `α/r` before being added in. Only the middle column changes below — the adapter's own answer is fixed once training ends.

![The alpha over r gain shown as three arithmetic examples. The frozen layer always outputs 5.0 and the adapter always outputs 0.3. With a gain of 0 the final output is 5.0, the base model unchanged. With gain 1 it is 5.3, the nudge as learned. With gain 2 it is 5.6, the same nudge twice as strong.](/images/finetuning/finetuning-alpha-gain.svg)

**Why divide by `r`?** Rank 8 sums 8 tables; rank 64 sums 64. More terms means a naturally louder branch, so a learning rate tuned at `r=8` would run too hot at `r=64`. Dividing by `r` normalizes that away, letting you change rank without re-tuning the learning rate. So the two knobs separate cleanly: **`r` = capacity, `α` = strength**, and `α = 2r` (a gain of 2) is the common convention.

The footgun follows directly, and it's silent: **raising `r` while leaving `α` fixed *weakens* the adapter.** Going `r=8 → r=64` at fixed `α` drops the gain from 2.0 to 0.25. Move them together. (One footnote for the `r ≥ 64` crowd: dividing by `r` over-damps at high rank; [rsLoRA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.03732) divides by `√r` instead and trains better there.)

A bonus that falls out of the gain being external to `A` and `B`: you can dial it *down* after training. That's what "LoRA at 0.7 weight" means in image generation, and it's how multiple adapters get blended.

## 6. Which layers get adapters

A transformer block is seven big matrix multiplies. The question is which ones receive an A/B pair.

![Which parts of a transformer block receive LoRA adapters. The four attention projections Q, K, V and O and the three MLP projections up, gate and down all receive adapters in modern practice. The 2021 paper adapted only Q and V, marked with stars. Normalization layers and embeddings receive none. Covering more layers tends to beat raising rank on fewer.](/images/finetuning/finetuning-target-modules.svg)

The 2021 paper adapted only **Q and V**, to demonstrate how little you could get away with. Later work (QLoRA especially) found that **spreading adapters across all seven matters more than raising rank on fewer.** Layer norms are skipped (negligible parameters) and embeddings are skipped (enormous, sensitive, rarely what needs to change). Modern default: `target_modules="all-linear"`.

## 7. BF16: reach versus fineness

Now the two number formats that show up in every QLoRA config. Every float format splits its bits between **range** (how huge or tiny before it breaks) and **precision** (how finely it divides).

![Comparison of FP32, FP16 and BF16 showing how each divides its bits between range and precision. FP32 uses 4 bytes with wide range and very wide precision. FP16 uses 2 bytes and shrinks range, so tiny numbers collapse to zero. BF16 uses 2 bytes but keeps FP32's full range, shrinking precision instead — half the memory, none of the fragility.](/images/finetuning/finetuning-bf16.svg)

BF16 wins for deep learning because it **exploits a strength while respecting a constraint.** Nets tolerate imprecise weights well (precision is cheap to give up) but cannot tolerate gradients vanishing to zero (range is non-negotiable). FP16 made the opposite trade and needs "loss scaling" hacks to survive. Note that BF16 is just the ambient training precision — **not part of LoRA's definition.** Mixed-precision training predates LoRA and applies equally to full fine-tuning.

## 8. NF4: put your levels where the data is

Quantization replaces each weight with the nearest entry in a small menu of allowed values. 4 bits buys a 16-item menu. The "N" is for **Normal**, as in the normal distribution.

![Uniform 4-bit levels versus NF4 levels placed against the bell-curve distribution of weights. Both panels show the same bell-shaped weight distribution with 16 ticks beneath. On the left the ticks are evenly spaced, so many fall in the empty tails describing weights that barely exist. On the right the NF4 ticks crowd near the centre where most weights actually sit, so the typical weight is described well.](/images/finetuning/finetuning-nf4.svg)

Neural-network weights are reliably bell-shaped, so NF4 places its 16 values **unevenly — crowded near zero where the weights actually live**, spread out in the near-empty tails. Same 4 bits, far less damage than uniform spacing. Three pieces of supporting machinery:

- **Block-wise scaling.** Weights are quantized in blocks of 64, each with its own scale, so one freak large weight distorts only its own block.
- **Double quantization.** Those per-block scales are themselves memory (~0.5 bits/weight), so compress them too → ~0.13 bits/weight. Saves ~3 GB on a 65B model. Unglamorous, free.
- **NF4 is a storage format, not a compute format.** Nothing is ever multiplied in NF4. A block is unpacked to BF16, used, and discarded.

## QLoRA = LoRA + an NF4 base

Put §7 and §8 together and you have QLoRA: **frozen and compressed in NF4; computed and trained in BF16.** NF4 is only viable *because* the base is frozen — you can afford lossy compression on something you'll never update, and rounding error in a read-only lookup doesn't accumulate. Three separate contributions get conflated constantly: **NF4** (compresses the frozen base), **double quantization** (compresses NF4's own metadata), and **paged optimizers** (unrelated plumbing that pages optimizer state to CPU RAM when VRAM spikes, instead of OOM-ing).

Here is the table that is the *actual* reason LoRA took over — 7B fine-tuning moved from a multi-GPU cluster to a single consumer card:

| 7B model | Full fine-tune | LoRA (r=8) | QLoRA (r=8) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weights | 14 GB | 14 GB frozen | **~4 GB** frozen |
| Gradients | 14 GB | ~50 MB | ~50 MB |
| Optimizer state | 56 GB | ~100 MB | ~100 MB |
| **Total** | **~84 GB** | **~15 GB** | **~5–6 GB** |

## 9. "Frozen" means not *updated*, not *uninvolved*

Here's the subtlety that trips people who assume frozen weights are free. The backward pass computes **two different things** at each layer:

![The backward pass travels through every frozen weight matrix but only produces updates for the adapters. Three layers each contain a frozen W and a trainable A and B pair. The forward pass moves left to right into the loss. The backward pass returns right to left along the full depth, branching upward into each layer's adapters to update A and B, while W is only read in order to pass the error signal along — and in QLoRA, unpacked from NF4 again on the way back.](/images/finetuning/finetuning-frozen-backward.svg)

1. **"How should I change my own weights?"** — needed only for trainable parameters. Skipped for frozen `W`. **This is the saving.**
2. **"How much did my *input* contribute to the error?"** — needed to hand the signal back to earlier layers, and computing it *requires* `W`'s values.

Job 2 is unavoidable, because there are adapters in layer 1: the error signal has to cross every layer to reach them, and crossing a layer means being multiplied by that layer's `W`. **`W` is a lens** — light passes through and is bent by it on every pass; nobody is grinding a new lens. The consequences people get wrong:

- **LoRA does not make backprop cheap in compute.** Full-depth forward and backward. Expect ~60–70% of full fine-tuning's step time, not 1%.
- **Activations still must be stored**, so memory still scales with batch size and sequence length. Gradient checkpointing remains useful under LoRA (Part 5 is what happens when you forget this on a T4).
- **QLoRA unpacks `W` twice per step** (forward and backward) — the source of its 20–30% slowdown. QLoRA *is* slower than LoRA, just not because "4-bit arithmetic is inefficient."

## 10. Merging is optional

During fine-tuning, **nothing merges.** The `+` adds the two branches' *output vectors*, not their weight matrices, so the NF4/BF16 format mismatch never even arises. At inference you pick one:

| Option | Trade-off |
|---|---|
| **Don't merge** | Run both branches. Small latency cost, lossless, allows hot-swapping and stacking. What most serving stacks do. |
| **Merge into the NF4 copy** | Unpack → add → re-quantize. **Lossy** — your learned delta gets snapped back onto the 16-value menu. A real QLoRA gotcha. |
| **Merge into the original BF16 checkpoint** | Clean, no re-quantization loss, gives a normal deployable model. The standard move. |

## 11. The config, annotated

The whole thing in ~20 lines. This is the exact shape the rest of the series runs (at `r=16`):

```python
import torch
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM, BitsAndBytesConfig
from peft import LoraConfig, get_peft_model, prepare_model_for_kbit_training

# --- the Q in QLoRA: how the frozen base is stored ---
bnb = BitsAndBytesConfig(
    load_in_4bit=True,
    bnb_4bit_quant_type="nf4",              # bell-curve-aware levels, not uniform
    bnb_4bit_use_double_quant=True,         # compress the block scales too
    bnb_4bit_compute_dtype=torch.bfloat16,  # what NF4 unpacks INTO for compute
)

model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(
    "meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B",
    quantization_config=bnb,
    torch_dtype=torch.bfloat16,
    device_map="auto",
)
model = prepare_model_for_kbit_training(model, use_gradient_checkpointing=True)

# --- the LoRA part: the A/B adapters ---
peft_config = LoraConfig(
    r=16,                        # capacity: how many rank-1 tables
    lora_alpha=32,               # strength: gain = alpha/r = 2.0
    lora_dropout=0.05,
    bias="none",
    task_type="CAUSAL_LM",
    target_modules="all-linear", # all seven projections, not just Q and V
)
model = get_peft_model(model, peft_config)
model.print_trainable_parameters()
# -> trainable: ~42M || all: ~8.0B || trainable%: 0.52
```

Merging afterward, the safe way — reload the base in **BF16, not the 4-bit version**, then merge:

```python
from peft import PeftModel

base = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained(
    "meta-llama/Llama-3.1-8B", torch_dtype=torch.bfloat16, device_map="cpu"
)
merged = PeftModel.from_pretrained(base, "out/adapter").merge_and_unload()
merged.save_pretrained("out/merged")
```

## 12. Higher-level takeaways

**LoRA as a design pattern.** It's a representation change — but unlike classic transform-and-conquer (FFT, Gaussian elimination, presorting), the transform is *not* equivalence-preserving. It **shrinks the solution space**: the best ΔW expressible as `B·A` is generally not the best ΔW. So LoRA is "deliberately solve a smaller surrogate problem, and bet the surrogate's optimum is close enough." The empirical finding that it usually *is* close enough is the entire contribution — the algebra was never the hard part.

**QLoRA as a space-time trade-off, run backwards.** Most space-time trades spend space to save time (memoization, lookup tables, indexes). QLoRA spends **time to save space** — same family as keeping data gzipped on disk. NF4 is the lossy-codec version of that instinct, allocating its 16 values where the data actually is, much as JPEG allocates bits where the eye actually looks.

**Why LoRA beat its competitors — modularity, not just memory.** Because the adapter sits *parallel* to `W` and combines by plain addition, it can be merged away to zero inference cost, or kept separate and hot-swapped: one base model in GPU memory serving many fine-tunes. Earlier PEFT methods lost exactly here — series adapters added permanent inference latency; prefix tuning consumed context window. **Memory savings got LoRA in the door; the deployment story is why it stayed.**

## The one-liner that ties it together

Distilled: **LoRA freezes `W` and learns a dense ΔW as `r` coordinated patterns (`B·A`) scaled by `α/r`; QLoRA additionally stores the frozen `W` in NF4 to fit it on one card — and the whole thing merges to a normal model, or doesn't, at your option.** Low-rank, not sparse; frozen, not uninvolved; cheap to train, cheap to deploy.
